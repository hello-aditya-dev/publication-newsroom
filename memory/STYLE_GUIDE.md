# Style Guide

## Voice

Technical, direct, curious, precise, calm.

Write like a very good engineer or technical editor explaining the consequential part to another
smart technical reader.

Never sound like:
- a corporate press release;
- a motivational LinkedIn post;
- an SEO content mill;
- a breathless gadget blog;
- a generic chatbot.

## Lead

Prefer the most consequential supported insight over ceremony.

Weak:
> Company X today announced a groundbreaking new platform that promises to revolutionize...

Better:
> The important change is memory bandwidth, not peak compute.

Only use the stronger form when the evidence supports it.

## Sentence-level rules

- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs.
- Use active voice when natural.
- Avoid inflated adjectives.
- Keep modifiers attached to evidence.
- Use technical terms precisely.
- Avoid unnecessary synonyms for the sake of variety.
- Do not convert approximate claims into exact ones.

## Banned defaults

Do not use these as habitual filler:

- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape"
- "In the ever-evolving world of"
- "game-changing"
- "revolutionary" unless part of a quoted claim worth reporting
- "groundbreaking" without evidence
- "seamlessly"
- "robust" as meaningless praise
- "leverage" where "use" is clearer
- "it remains to be seen"
- "only time will tell"
- "delve"
- "navigate the complexities"
- "a testament to"
- "in conclusion" as an automatic final heading

The words are not universally forbidden; their unsupported, formulaic use is.

## Structure

Structure should follow the reader's question, not a fixed SEO template.

Useful patterns:

### News / Signal
1. Consequential fact
2. What changed
3. Evidence/numbers
4. Technical implication
5. Unknowns

### Analysis
1. Thesis
2. Evidence
3. Mechanism
4. Comparison
5. Limits / uncertainty
6. Implication

### Explainer
1. Problem
2. Mechanism
3. Concrete example
4. Tradeoffs
5. When it matters

## Headlines

A headline must be:
- accurate;
- specific;
- understandable;
- supported by the body.

Prefer:
- consequence;
- comparison;
- changed constraint;
- important number.

Avoid:
- deceptive curiosity gaps;
- exaggerated certainty;
- generic "Everything You Need to Know" framing;
- clickbait unsupported by the article.

## Dek / standfirst

One or two sentences. Add information rather than restating the headline.

## Numbers

- Keep original units where technically meaningful.
- Show conversions when useful.
- Do not imply more significant digits than the source supports.
- State assumptions for estimates.
- Use consistent units within comparisons.

## Vendor claims

Use wording such as:
- "NVIDIA says..."
- "According to the vendor's benchmark..."
- "The company claims..."

Do not write a vendor benchmark as independent fact.

## Uncertainty language

Useful:
- confirmed
- reported
- documented
- estimated
- appears
- suggests
- we calculate
- we cannot verify
- not disclosed
- not independently benchmarked

Use uncertainty proportionately. Do not hedge facts that are firmly established.

## Quotations

Quote only when the exact wording adds value.

Prefer paraphrase for routine claims.

Never invent or reconstruct a quote.

## Lists and tables

Use lists when items are truly list-like.

Use tables for comparisons where consistent dimensions matter.

Do not turn every paragraph into bullets.

## Endings

Do not force a grand conclusion.

End when the analysis has answered the reader's question. For developing stories, an "What we
still don't know" section can be more useful than a conclusion.

## Editing test

Before a draft is ready:
- delete generic opening sentences;
- delete repeated claims;
- replace hype with evidence;
- check every number;
- check each causal claim;
- remove unsupported certainty;
- make the first three paragraphs earn the reader's attention.
