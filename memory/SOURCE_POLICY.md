# Source Policy

## Principle

Use the strongest available evidence for each claim.

Source quality depends on the claim. A vendor is authoritative about what it announced, but not
automatically authoritative about comparative superiority.

## Source tiers

### Tier A — Primary / authoritative

Examples:
- official product documentation;
- specifications;
- standards;
- source code and release notes;
- peer-reviewed papers and original preprints;
- company engineering posts;
- regulatory filings;
- government advisories;
- official incident reports;
- earnings materials;
- conference papers/slides from the originating team.

Use these for material factual claims whenever possible.

### Tier B — High-quality independent reporting / analysis

Examples:
- reputable technical journalism;
- established security research organizations;
- independent benchmark organizations;
- expert analysis with transparent methodology.

Use for:
- independent context;
- corroboration;
- interviews;
- competitive framing;
- claims not available from primary sources.

### Tier C — Specialist commentary

Examples:
- experienced practitioner blogs;
- technical newsletters;
- conference commentary;
- credible analyst notes.

Useful for:
- story discovery;
- hypotheses;
- identifying questions.

Verify material facts elsewhere.

### Tier D — Social / unverified

Examples:
- social posts;
- forums;
- anonymous claims;
- screenshots without provenance.

Use as a lead, not as proof.

## Claim-source fit

A source's authority is contextual.

Examples:

- A company spec sheet can prove what specification it publishes.
- It cannot independently prove that its product is "2× better" under real-world workloads.
- A benchmark can support only the conditions it actually tested.
- A paper's abstract should not substitute for reading methodology when the methodology matters.

## Minimum sourcing

### Signal

At least one strong primary source for the central event when available.

### Breakdown / Deep Dive

Prefer multiple independent sources and primary evidence. No fixed number substitutes for source
quality.

## Source record

Research dossiers should capture:

- title;
- URL;
- organization/author;
- source tier;
- original publication/update date when available;
- access date;
- what claims it supports;
- limitations or conflicts.

## Search snippets

Search snippets are discovery aids.

Do not cite a snippet as the evidentiary basis for a consequential claim when the underlying page
can be opened.

## Secondary-reporting ethics

Do not paraphrase an entire competitor story into a substitute article.

When another publication has original reporting:
- attribute the reporting;
- link to it in research notes;
- add independent value;
- do not erase the origin of the scoop.

## Source contradictions

When reliable sources disagree:
1. record both;
2. identify whether they measure/define different things;
3. seek a more authoritative source;
4. preserve uncertainty if unresolved.

## Versioning

For rapidly changing docs, record access date and, when possible, version/release number.

## Paywalled sources

Do not evade paywalls. Use legitimately accessible material.
